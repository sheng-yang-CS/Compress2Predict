Try to be unpredictable.

Type 0s and 1s. Before each bit, an algorithm has already guessed it. You will build predictors like this one during the project, and prove how good they can be.

A language model does the same job with words instead of bits. It predicts the next one. This project studies the case where we can prove everything.

A fair coin costs the machine exactly 1 bit per symbol. Every pattern in your sequence makes it cheaper. Predicting well and compressing well are the same task.

Two predictors play against you. KT only counts: after n symbols with n1 ones, it gives the next 1 the probability (n1 + ½) / (n + 1). This is the +½ rule. CTW also looks at the bits you played just before.

Keyboard: press 0 or 1. The machine starts knowing nothing, so the first symbols are expensive. That extra cost is called redundancy, and it is the subject of this project.

The idea

A good compressor is a good predictor

Give a probability \(Q(y_i \mid y_1 \dots y_{i-1})\) to each next symbol. An arithmetic coder turns these probabilities into a file of about \(-\log_2 Q(y_1 \dots y_n)\) bits. The better the predictions, the shorter the file.

If you know the true law \(P_\theta\) of the source, you use it and you reach the entropy, which is Shannon's limit. The interesting case is when you do not know \(\theta\). The extra bits you pay are the redundancy:

\[ R_n(\theta, Q) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{P_\theta}\!\left[\log_2 \frac{P_\theta(Y^n)}{Q(Y^n)}\right] \;=\; D\!\left(P_\theta^n \,\|\, Q\right) \;\ge\; 0 . \]

The first question of the project: how small can the redundancy be, and which \(Q\) achieves it?

For a coin of unknown bias, the answer is a rule you can state in one line. After \(n\) symbols, of which \(n_1\) are ones, predict

\[ Q(\text{next} = 1 \mid y_1 \dots y_n) \;=\; \frac{n_1 + \tfrac12}{n + 1} . \]

This is the +½ rule, also called KT after Krichevsky and Trofimov. Why ½ and not 0 or 1 is one of the first things you will prove.

The maths you will do

Probability, analysis, combinatorics, and one algorithm

Everything in the first months uses tools from your preparatory classes. Each one answers a concrete question here.

Probability

The law of large numbers, applied to the logarithm of a probability, makes entropy appear as a limit.

\[ -\frac1n \log_2 P_\theta(Y_1,\dots,Y_n) \;\longrightarrow\; H(P_\theta) \]

Then Bayes' rule, Beta laws, and Markov chains with a memory of variable length.

Analysis

Average the Bernoulli law over \(\theta\) with weight \(1/\sqrt{\theta(1-\theta)}\). A Beta integral gives a closed form.

\[ Q_{\mathrm{KT}}(y^n) \;=\; \frac{\Gamma\!\left(n_1+\tfrac12\right)\Gamma\!\left(n_0+\tfrac12\right)}{\pi\,\Gamma(n+1)} \]

Stirling's formula then shows that its redundancy is \(\tfrac12\log_2 n + O(1)\). Convexity and Jensen's inequality give \(D \ge 0\).

Combinatorics

Count the sequences with \(k\) ones among \(n\).

\[ \binom{n}{k} \;\approx\; 2^{\,n\,h(k/n)} \]

Kraft's inequality says which lengths \(\ell_i\) a prefix code can have.

\[ \sum_{i} 2^{-\ell_i} \;\le\; 1 \]

Put together: naming one sequence among \(N\) takes \(\log_2 N\) bits, so a sequence with \(k\) ones costs about \(n\,h(k/n)\) bits. Entropy appears a second time, with no probability at all.

Algorithms

The number of context trees grows doubly exponentially with the depth D, roughly like 1.52D. At depth 6 there are already more than 200 billion. One recursion sums over all of them in linear time.

\[ P_w^{\,s} \;=\; \tfrac12\,P_e^{\,s} \;+\; \tfrac12\,P_w^{\,0s}\,P_w^{\,1s} \]

This is the context-tree direction of the project. You prove this identity, program it, and check it against brute force on small trees.

Roadmap

A common core, then one direction

About 25 days in the lab, one day a week. The first ten sessions are the same whatever comes next. Around January we choose one direction together, once you know the tools and your own taste.

The core is a complete piece of work in itself. Each step has something to prove, something to program, and something to show.

Core, part 1 · about 4 sessions

Key notions

Then one direction, about 15 sessions

Four examples of where the year can go. All of them use the two objects of the core, a divergence and a mixture. The year ends with a report and a talk at the group seminar.

Context trees

How do you predict a source whose memory length is unknown?

Prove that one recursion computes a mixture over all trees, and bound its redundancy. Program binary CTW and test it on text against gzip. The tree above belongs to this direction.

Model selection

How many parameters does the data justify?

Each parameter costs about ½ log₂ n bits. Prove that this turns code length into a rule for choosing a model. Program it to find the order of a Markov chain from data.

Testing and large deviations

How fast can you tell two laws apart?

Count sequences by their type, then prove Sanov's theorem and Stein's lemma on a finite alphabet. Program tests and compare their error rates with the predicted exponent.

Limits of estimation

What can no estimator do?

Prove Fano's inequality and a first minimax lower bound. Program estimators on a large alphabet, and estimate the probability of symbols never seen so far.

Can this lead to a publication?

Yes, it can. If the results are good, the work can be written up as a research paper, usually a short conference paper or a preprint. This takes extra work beyond the one day a week, and it often continues into a second year.

Horizon

Questions that are still open

Each direction leads to questions nobody has fully answered. Three examples.

What has a language model learned? A language model predicts the next word and is judged by log-loss, like every predictor on this page. For sources with a tree-shaped memory we know the best possible predictor exactly. How close does a trained model come to it, and how fast? What a context-tree predictor and a language model share, and where they differ?

A very large alphabet. With \(m\) symbols, the cost of each context is bounded by about \(\tfrac{m-1}{2}\log_2 n\) bits. For tens of thousands of words this bound is too large to be useful. Which estimators pay less when only a few symbols really occur?

Data that follow no model. Real data are not drawn from any law in your family. Which guarantees survive? Bounds that hold for every individual sequence give one answer, and they are far from complete.
